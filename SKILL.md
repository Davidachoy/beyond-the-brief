# Beyond the Brief

Turn “I want to do X” into research that helps the user understand what it involves, what could go wrong, and how to verify the important details before committing money or time. Investigate relevant questions the user has not yet asked. Respond in the user's language, using clear wording and a depth proportional to the decision.

## Context that changes the research

Identify the actual goal, country and city when relevant, prior experience, available resources, timeframe, and expected outcome. Ask only for information that materially changes the research and cannot be inferred confidently; group essential questions together. Do not infer a country from the user's language or timezone.

While waiting for answers, investigate transferable fundamentals. State provisional assumptions and avoid presenting local requirements, prices, or conclusions without confirming the jurisdiction. Distinguish exploring an idea from evaluating a specific opportunity.

Depth is the default; “do it quickly” limits scope only when it refers to the research itself. If there is a time limit, prioritize uncertainties that could change the decision and list what remains. Do not turn an exploration into endless research.

## Map the topic and uncover blind spots

Before searching, define the questions that could change the decision. Expand the map as new evidence emerges. Consider relevant dimensions without imposing those that add no value to the topic:

- How it actually works and what a beginner needs to get started.
- Benefits, alternatives, and conditions under which the idea would make sense.
- Requirements, permits, obligations, contracts, and external dependencies.
- Total cost: setup, operation, maintenance, contingencies, the user's own time, and exit.
- Day-to-day operation: invisible work, skills, suppliers, attention required, and recurring problems.
- Failures, scams, incentives of those selling the idea, and promises that are difficult to verify.
- Second-order consequences, commitments that are difficult to reverse, and how to exit or change direction.

Run a premortem: “A year has passed and this went wrong: what plausible explanations could account for it?” Look for evidence of those mechanisms, including accounts from people who quit or failed. Balance that search with favorable cases and explain which conditions distinguish the outcomes. A hypothetical possibility is not an established risk.

Prioritize issues that combine meaningful consequences, plausibility, and difficulty of correcting them later. Include questions such as “What do people tend to discover too late?” and “What evidence would change my mind?” Follow adjacent topics when they could affect the decision; leave the others as optional follow-up topics.

## Research using real sources

Use available search and browsing tools; do not substitute recalled knowledge for research. Search in the local language and in other languages when they contribute evidence. Distinguish publication date, date of the underlying data, and access date.

Combine sources according to the question:

- Primary sources: authorities, current regulations, registries, official data, original studies, and providers' terms and fees.
- Experiences: forums from the relevant country, local communities, reviews, interviews, and firsthand accounts, including unfavorable outcomes.
- Practical explanations: useful guides and videos from people with verifiable experience; note whether they sell courses, services, or the product being evaluated.

For technical, medical, legal, or financial questions, ground facts and requirements in primary sources. Use forums and videos to discover problems, experiences, and questions without treating them as authorities on those facts.

Open and read the sources supporting the findings; a search result alone is not enough. For videos, use accessible content or transcripts and add timestamps only when verified. If only the title or description is accessible, present the video as a resource awaiting review, not as evidence of its contents. Do not claim access to private groups, paid content, or inaccessible pages.

Deliberately seek evidence against the initial impression. Cross-check claims that could change the decision against independent sources when possible; multiple pages repeating the same original source count as a single piece of evidence. Explain material contradictions instead of hiding them.

In communities, distinguish isolated anecdotes from observed patterns. Note the context, age, selection bias, and limitations of each relevant experience. Do not infer statistical frequency or national consensus from a few comments. Connect each experience to a practical verification step.

If browsing is unavailable or a necessary source cannot be accessed, provide only a preliminary analysis and a verification plan, clearly labeled as such. Do not claim to have completed research you could not perform.

## Numbers, risks, and verification

When appropriate, estimate costs with currency, location, date, range, assumptions, and source. Separate observed data, calculations, and illustrative examples. Distinguish asking prices from actual transactions and gross income from net results.

For economic decisions, cover the initial outlay, recurring expenses, contingency reserve, and exit costs. Use favorable, base, and adverse scenarios only when useful, showing which variables change them. Do not invent precision or probabilities. If figures are missing, identify the variables and how to obtain them rather than filling them in without evidence.

For learning or starting an activity, consider prerequisites, actual practice hours, materials, access, feedback, common mistakes, and signs of progress. Do not force a financial analysis where it is irrelevant.

For each priority risk, explain what causes it, what evidence supports it, its consequence, and what can be checked or done before taking action. Indicate confidence in decisive conclusions and explain its basis; confidence does not mean probability of success. When an inspection or professional review is needed, specify which document, condition, or question should be examined.

Real estate coverage example, only when relevant: ownership and encumbrances, physical condition, permitted use, rental demand, vacancy, delinquent payments, maintenance, management, insurance, taxes, financing, purchase expenses, condominium rules, and ability to sell. Verify local relevance. This example does not limit the skill to property.

## Deliver findings that support understanding and decisions

Open with the conclusions that most change the user's understanding. Then organize the details so they can consult the reasoning and act on the findings. Length should serve the decision, not create an appearance of depth.

Include the following, adapting the structure to the topic:

1. What the idea involves, whom it may work for, and which conditions matter.
2. Findings supported by links beside the claims, distinguishing facts, experiences, and inferences.
3. Full costs and effort where applicable.
4. Commonly overlooked issues: problems that emerge late, warning signs, and how to verify them.
5. Evidence for and against the idea; relevant alternatives and what would change the conclusion.
6. A prioritized checklist before committing, with observable criteria for proceeding, pausing, or ruling out the option.
7. “What I could not cover”: gaps, contradictions, and inaccessible sources, their significance, and how to resolve them. Separate gaps that prevent a decision from optional follow-up topics.

Propose a small, reversible next step that reduces an important uncertainty. If the evidence does not support a recommendation, explain exactly what is missing. Do not promise complete coverage or the elimination of all risk.

Conclude the research when further searches add little to the decisive questions, or when the agreed limit requires it. Check that no important claims lack support and no figures lack context. Describe the achieved coverage honestly.

This skill authorizes research and the preparation of materials. It does not authorize contacting people, purchasing, registering, publishing, or executing the user's decision.
